## The decision framework

One question leads: **what serves our customers best?** The customer sets the target. The inputs set the path.

Eight inputs, taken in order. The numbers are a path, not a ranking. No input carries a fixed weight.

1. **The team.** The people who own what the decision touches, asked before the decision is framed.
2. **Evidence.** What employees, customers, and end users are telling us.
3. **Category.** The market it plays in, what it can hold, what it can charge.
4. **Technology.** What it is built on and what a choice costs. A shared platform does not decide a brand.
5. **Resources.** What it takes to run as its own brand.
6. **Equity.** What the name is already worth, asked per audience.
7. **Marketing.** What it needs to be sold.
8. **Reversibility.** What it costs to change our mind.

Three outputs: **Brand**, **Product**, or **Feature**. Traps that never decide: attachment, sunk work, championship, price paid, platform.

Before the call is made, the checklist at the end of section 03 on the page runs in full. The decision, the date, who made it, and the reasoning get recorded here.

## How to record a decision

When the framework produces a call, three edits happen at once: add a one-line entry to the decisions table above, fill in a decision record below using this template, and remove anything the decision settles from Proposed or Not decided. Records stack newest first.

### Decision record template

- **Date:**
- **What was on the table:** The company, product, or brand in question, and what triggered the look.
- **The call:** Brand, Product, or Feature.
- **Decided by:**
- **What the customer gets:** The answer to the question that leads.
- **The inputs that mattered most:** Two or three, and what each said.
- **What almost changed the answer:** The strongest argument against, and why it lost.
- **Reversibility:** What it would cost to change this later.
- **Communication:** Which sequence variant runs.

## Proposed, awaiting review

Added 2026-08-06 by Studio Parkside so the page could be completed for review. Each of these is a recommendation, not a decision. They move to the decisions table when Paul, Snug, and Janet confirm them, or they get replaced by whatever the review lands on.

1. **The team as input 01.** Checking with the people who own what a decision touches is its own numbered step, first in the framework. Proposed roles: product, engineering, data, payments, finance, marketing, sales, client success.
2. **The diligence circle.** Finance from the first conversation. Product and engineering once a deal is plausible. Data and payments before terms are set. Marketing and brand before terms are set. Legal throughout.
3. **Sequence variants by acquisition shape.** Camp brand: all seven steps, about eight weeks. Capability tuck-in: steps 01 to 04 in about a week, three to four weeks total. Vertical next door: all seven steps, six to eight weeks. Vertical that does not obviously fit: narrative first, about twelve weeks.
4. **Footer treatment.** Text only, no logo lockup, until the parent has a name and a mark.
5. **Social ownership.** Each brand's marketing owner runs its accounts. Posts touching a brand change get parent-level marketing approval. Acquired accounts stay quiet until their plan is set.
6. **Trade show lead times.** Booth and print to vendors eight weeks out. Talking points to booth staff two weeks out. Show lists kept per brand by each marketing lead.
7. **Two narratives.** A customer-facing version for what a change means, and a version for people joining from an acquired company. Draft language is on the page, marked proposed.
8. **Where this file lives.** Beside the page. Studio Parkside holds the pen through activation, then ownership moves in-house to whoever owns marketing.
9. **Budget working assumption.** Until Campminder's own figures arrive, the 8% of revenue median stands in as the marketing denominator.

## Why the architecture matters

Figures carried over from the July strategy work. Sources are named but have not been re-verified against primary documents.

- **McKinsey:** strong-brand B2B companies see EBIT margins about 20% higher than weak-brand peers.
- **Kantar BrandZ:** strong brands outperformed the S&P 500 by 82 points over two decades, and fell less and recovered faster through both the 2008 and 2020 downturns.
- **LinkedIn:** companies with strong employer brands see up to 43% lower cost per hire.
- **Kantar:** brands that improved pricing power added 67% to brand value over four years, roughly twice the gain of everyone else.
