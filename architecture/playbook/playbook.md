## The decision framework

One question leads: **what serves our customers best?** The customer sets the target. The inputs set the path.

### Two vocabularies, kept separate

- **Tier** describes what a thing *is* today: **Brand**, **Product**, or **Feature**. Section 02-03 of the page.
- **Outcome** is what the framework *decides*: **Keep**, **Absorb**, **Rebrand**, or **Retire**. Section 04-01 of the page.

A tier is a description. An outcome is a decision. Do not use one where the other belongs.

**This is a lens, not a rulebook.** The same inputs can point different ways, and a good reason can override any single line. It is the thing that stops the framework being run mechanically, so it belongs in any room where a decision is actually being made.

### Inputs

Two lead, and they lead on purpose.

1. **Customers.** What do the people who use this actually want, and what would they miss if it changed? Every other input is weighed underneath this one. Asked of employees, customers, and end users alike.
2. **Our team.** Leadership and every department lead, asked before the decision is framed rather than after. Checking with the people who own what a decision touches is its own step, ahead of the remaining inputs. Roles: product, engineering, data, payments, finance, marketing, sales, client success, legal.

Then six, in no fixed weight order. Which one matters most changes with the decision, and a scorecard would move the argument from the decision to the math.

3. **Category.** The market it plays in, what it can hold, what it can charge, whether it is growing.
4. **Technology.** What it is built on and what a choice costs. A shared platform never decides a brand. Two brands can run on one codebase, and one brand can run on two.
5. **Resources.** Engineering and product upkeep, support load, finance and HR time.
6. **Equity.** What the name is already worth, asked once per audience. A name can be strong with parents and unknown to buyers.
7. **Marketing.** Its own site and social presence, its own sales approach, its own booth.
8. **Reversibility.** What it costs to change our mind, and what this forces us to decide next.

### Watchouts

Four are on the page: **attachment**, **sunk work**, **price paid**, **platform**. A fifth also applies and lives here rather than on the page's summary card, for space: **who championed it**. None of these tell us what a thing is worth to the portfolio.

### Before the call

The six-item checklist in section 04-03 of the page runs in full: customers, our team, other inputs, watchouts avoided, single answer, decision shared. An empty box is work to complete. The decision, the date, who made it, and the reasoning get recorded below.

## How to record a decision

When the framework produces a call, two edits happen at once: add a one-line entry to the decisions table above, and fill in a decision record below using this template. Records stack newest first.

### Decision record template

- **Date:**
- **What was on the table:** The company, product, or brand in question, and what triggered the look.
- **The call:** Keep, Absorb, Rebrand, or Retire.
- **Resulting tier, if it changes:** Brand, Product, or Feature.
- **Decided by:**
- **What the customer gets:** The answer to the question that leads.
- **The inputs that mattered most:** Two or three, and what each said.
- **What almost changed the answer:** The strongest argument against, and why it lost.
- **Reversibility:** What it would cost to change this later.
- **Communication:** Which sequence variant runs.

### Decision records

The entries in the table above from July 16 and August 3 predate this template and are captured there in short form. The record below is the first full write-up, and the pattern for every one after it.

**House of Brands model**
- **Date:** 2026-07-16
- **What was on the table:** Whether Campminder's portfolio operates as a house of brands, with each brand keeping its own name and market, or consolidates toward a single shared brand.
- **The call:** Keep, for every brand in the house. Each keeps its own name and market. A parent company holds the portfolio without selling software itself.
- **Resulting tier, if it changes:** No change. Campminder, UltraCamp, Swimtopia, and Gazebo remain Brands.
- **Decided by:** Leadership.
- **What the customer gets:** Camp directors, swim coaches, and event managers keep working with the name and product they already picked, with a larger company standing behind it rather than replacing it.
- **The inputs that mattered most:** Customers, since each brand serves an audience with real equity in its own name. Category, since camp, swim, and event management are different enough markets that one shared brand would blur the pitch to each of them.
- **What almost changed the answer:** A single company-wide brand would have been simpler to build and market. That argument didn't hold once weighed against what each brand's customers would lose, and against the fact that the platforms underneath these brands were already converging into one codebase regardless of what the brands did. Shared technology tells us what a choice costs, not what the choice is.
- **Reversibility:** Low. Undoing a house of brands after customers have built loyalty to individual names costs more than making the call carefully the first time.
- **Communication:** Ran the full sequence in section 05, starting with leadership before anything reached a customer.

## Detail that lives here, not on the page

A playbook is the actions you take. The reasoning behind them is reference, and reference lives here. The page tells you what each brand is and what to do next. This file tells you why each one sits where it does, which is what you need when the framework gets run against it again.

### Why each brand sits where it does

These are the framework's outputs applied to the house as it stands today. The page states the tier. This is the argument behind it.

| Brand | Tier | Why it sits there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Campminder | Brand | Carries its own identity and is sold on its own. The deepest equity in the camp market, and the name our customers know best. |
| Campanion | Product | You have to have Campminder to use it, which places it under that brand rather than beside it. It carries strong recognition with families, and that recognition is worth protecting where it sits. A product can hold real equity without that equity making it a brand. |
| UltraCamp | Brand | Its own name, its own customers, and camps that chose it deliberately. That equity sits with the people who use it, which is what keeps it a brand of its own rather than a line inside Campminder. |
| Gazebo | Brand | A different audience from camp. Event managers are their own market with their own needs, and a brand that speaks to them directly does that better than a line inside someone else's product. |
| Swimtopia | Brand | A different sport, a different buyer, and a name with its own following. A camp director and a swim coach should not be sold the same product. |

The Swimtopia line is the House of Brands argument in miniature. When someone asks why the company does not consolidate to one name, that is the shortest honest answer.

### What the parent is for

Parent Co. exists so the people who build places where a community can gather get tools that are a pleasure to use, and so no single brand has to carry that on its own.

On who works there: people who grew up in this industry and people who chose it on purpose. Engineering, product, support, finance, and operations sit behind every brand rather than inside any one of them, which is what lets a camp director and a swim coach each get the full weight of the company behind the product they picked.

### Where a new acquisition lands

Four shapes cover almost everything we would buy. Each runs the same inputs and comes out somewhere predictable, which is what makes the first conversation short.

| Shape | What it is | Where it lands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Another camp brand | Selling to the same camps and camp directors we already serve. | A **Keep** at first, as its own brand. Equity and category decide whether it stays one. |
| B. Something our brands would use | A capability that plugs into what we already sell, the way Campanion plugs into Campminder. | An **Absorb**, landing as a product or a feature depending on whether it can be sold on its own. |
| C. A vertical next door | A different audience with the same shape of problem, the way swim sits next to camp. | A **Keep**. Its own brand under Parent Co., because its market is not ours. |
| D. A vertical that does not obviously fit | Makes sense to own but does not read as camp or swim from the outside. | A **Keep**, and the work is in the narrative. Lead with the people it serves and the moments they make, not the category we are known for. |

### Sequence variants by shape

The sequence in section 05 of the page runs for every change. The shape sets which steps carry weight and how far out it starts.

- **A. Another camp brand.** All steps in full, with the top-accounts call running heaviest, because customers overlap and reps get asked about both names on one call. Starts about eight weeks out.
- **B. Something our brands would use.** Board through brand leaders compress into about a week. Customer steps fold into normal product communication and the public step is a short announcement rather than a campaign. Starts three to four weeks out.
- **C. A vertical next door.** All steps, with extra weight on brand leaders and department briefings, since a new market has its own language and its own press. The acquired brand keeps its own voice externally. Starts six to eight weeks out.
- **D. A vertical that does not obviously fit.** The longest runway. The narrative gets written and tested inside before anything external is scheduled. Starts about twelve weeks out.

Branding work and communication deliverables slot into these steps rather than running alongside them. Emails, decks, branded accessories, trade show materials, and site updates each land at the step where they are needed, and which of them are needed at all depends on the determination.

### The diligence circle

A small group gets pulled in while the decision is still being made, because the answer depends on what they know. Payments, data, engineering, and finance each change the shape of a deal before it is a deal.

- Finance, from the first conversation. Nothing gets shaped without the numbers.
- Product and engineering, once a deal is plausible. They say what the platform can carry and what it costs to carry it.
- Data and payments, before terms are set. Moving customer records and money changes the price of a deal.
- Marketing and brand, before terms are set, so brand and communication costs sit in the deal model instead of surfacing after close.
- Legal, throughout.

The decision is made through the framework before a single word of the sequence goes out.

### Parent company visibility

Barely visible, on purpose. Customers buy Campminder and Swimtopia. The parent carries the story to investors, founders considering a sale, press, and the people we want to hire. On customer-facing brands it reads as a line in the footer, the way you find out a brand belongs to a larger group and then go back to what you were doing.

Footer treatment is text only, no logo lockup. Text is quieter, faster to roll out, and easy to upgrade to a lockup later.

### The picture is not fixed

A brand's role gets looked at against the framework whenever something material changes, and at least once a year. That is true of every name in the house, including Campminder. Writing the criteria down ahead of time is what turns a change into a decision people can follow rather than one that lands out of nowhere. **Keep is an answer, not a default.**

### Channel detail beyond the page summaries

- **Website.** Every brand site carries a footer line linking to the parent. The parent site never sells software. Careers live there, and if you join any brand here you join Parent Co.
- **Email.** Planning starts the day the decision is made, not the week of the announcement.
- **Sales.** The channel to protect. Whatever the team says first becomes the story. One shared answer, carried as a slide in the standard deck or a line in an existing presentation, never a new deck. Two questions come up more than any others and both need a ready answer: "are you getting bought" and "is my product going away." The page states this in general terms. The literal questions are here because they are what actually gets asked.
- **Current customers.** Calls for top accounts, email for everyone else, in person where a visit is already planned. Support gets the language before the email goes out, because they field the reply.
- **Social.** Each brand's marketing owner runs its accounts day to day. Posts touching a brand change get parent-level marketing approval. Parent Co. runs its own presence, LinkedIn first. Review Instagram, LinkedIn, TikTok, Facebook, and Reddit for each brand. An acquired brand's accounts get a plan, whether that is keeping, merging, or retiring them, and post nothing about the change until the plan is set.
- **PR.** Sometimes the acquired brand sends its own wire release alongside the parent's.
- **Events.** Camp brands anchor on national and regional camp conferences. Swim and event brands run their own circuits. Booth and print go to vendors eight weeks out, talking points to booth staff two weeks out, and each marketing lead keeps the show list for their brand.
- **Decks and templates.** Anything carrying a logo gets a pass whenever a name changes: slide decks, sales materials, merchandise, email signatures, templates. Budget for it as a longer list than anyone expects, because it always is. That warning is off the page and belongs in any rollout estimate.
- **Contracts and invoices.** Checks, invoices, contracts, and payment processing. Wherever a company name lands in front of a customer it has to match what that customer was told it would say, which means the finance and legal side of a name change runs on the same clock as the marketing side, not behind it.

### Narrative variants

Six audience-specific versions of the same story live in section 06 of the page: our team, new employees, partners, industry events, new customers, and current customers. Same story, shaped for how each audience meets us. Use them as written rather than improvising a seventh.

## Budget

Budgets are expressed as percentages, not line items, so the framework holds with any brand in the portfolio. The chain runs revenue, then marketing, then brand.

- **Marketing budget:** 10% of revenue.
- **Brand spend:** 20% of the marketing budget.

Brand spend then splits three ways:

- **20%** discovery and strategy. Interviews with the employees and customers of the brand in question, plus outside help when we need answers our own team cannot give us.
- **45%** brand and creative. Naming, identity, and the parent brand itself, plus the templates, decks, and systems the team reuses long after a project closes.
- **35%** rollout. Website, email, social, PR, sales enablement, and trade show materials.

The split is set once a year and spent against as things come up.

Acquisitions carry their own brand and communication line inside the integration budget, 5% to 20% of it depending on whether a name changes.

### Rollout carries product time

The rollout line covers website, email, social, PR, sales enablement, and trade show materials. It also carries product and engineering time, which is the line most often left out of an estimate.

### Budget benchmarks

Published figures, with sources, so the numbers above can be checked against the market.

| Figure | Value | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Marketing spend, private B2B SaaS | 8% of revenue, median. Equity-backed companies spend roughly 2x bootstrapped. | SaaS Capital, 2026 Spending Benchmarks for Private B2B SaaS Companies (1,000+ companies surveyed) |
| Marketing spend, cross-industry | 7.8% of revenue | Gartner 2026 CMO Spend Survey (n=401, mostly $1B+ revenue). Directional only, sample skews enterprise. |
| M&A integration cost, technology / media / telecom | Above 5.6% of the acquired company's revenue, median. Rebranding named as a cost driver, not separately quantified. | EY-Parthenon, 2024. 236 deals of $500M+ closed 2010–2023. |
| Brand as a share of marketing budget | No reliable public benchmark. | — |
| Brand cost as a share of deal value | No credible published benchmark exists. Do not use this framing. | — |

The 10% marketing line sits just above the 8% B2B SaaS median, which keeps the cascade's math simple and leaves room for a growing portfolio.

## Why the architecture matters

Figures carried over from the July strategy work.

- **McKinsey:** strong-brand B2B companies see EBIT margins about 20% higher than weak-brand peers.
- **Kantar BrandZ:** strong brands outperformed the S&P 500 by 82 points over two decades, and fell less and recovered faster through both the 2008 and 2020 downturns.
- **LinkedIn:** companies with strong employer brands see up to 43% lower cost per hire.
- **Kantar:** brands that improved pricing power added 67% to brand value over four years, roughly twice the gain of everyone else.
