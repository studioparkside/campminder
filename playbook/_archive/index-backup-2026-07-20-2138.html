A House of Brands.

  • Campminder stays Campminder.
  • Parent Co. holds the portfolio and carries the acquisition story.
  • Every brand keeps its own name and speaks to its own market.

The rest of this page is how we keep that straight.

One parent. Independent brands.

In a House of Brands, the parent company stands behind fully independent brands. Customers buy from the name they know and trust. The parent signals shared ownership, shared talent, and shared ambition. PepsiCo and Meta run this way.

Three reasons this is the right model for Campminder.

01

Campminder has real equity.

Customers keep the name they trust. Parent Co. adds a layer above Campminder instead of replacing it.

02

The brands serve different markets.

A Campminder customer and a Swimtopia customer have different needs and different reasons to buy. Each brand speaks to its own market without compromise.

03

The company already works this way.

Shared engineering, shared finance, and shared leadership already run beneath multiple product names. The model gives that structure a name and makes it visible.

04

How we decide.

Something new arrives. We buy a company, or we build something worth naming. The same question gets asked every time. What is this to the portfolio?

The framework below answers it the same way every time, no matter who is deciding. One question leads. Seven more get weighed under it. The answer sets the name, the identity, and the budget.

This is a lens, not a rulebook. The same inputs can point different ways, and a real reason can override any single line.

The customer decides. Everything else is a constraint.

One question sits above the seven. What do the people who use this actually want, and what would they miss if it changed.

Product tells us what is buildable, and when. Cost tells us what is worth carrying. Equity tells us what is risky to touch. All three shape the answer. None of them makes the call alone.

A build that is easy but that no customer asked for is not a reason to ship it. A name that is awkward but that customers know is not a reason to drop it. The customer sets the target. The rest sets the path.

What goes in.

01
Evidence

Interviews with our employees, with the people who use it, and with the families and members they serve.

02
Market

How much share this can command, what it can charge, and whether the category is growing.

03
Cost to carry

What it takes to run it as a brand. Engineering, marketing, support, HR, and finance.

04
Equity

Whether a name already exists that customers trust, search for, and would miss.

05
Audience overlap

Whether it serves someone we already serve. No overlap is the whole argument for the house.

06
Channel

Whether it needs its own sales motion, or rides one we already run.

07
Reversibility

How hard this is to undo if we get it wrong, and what it costs to change our mind later.

Room to add one. The framework grows as we learn what we missed.

What comes out.

Brand

Its own name, identity, and audience. Sold on its own. Joins the house.

In the house today: Campminder. Swimtopia. UltraCamp.

Sub-brand

Carries its own name alongside an existing brand and borrows its trust. Never introduced on its own.

In the house today: None yet.

Product

Its own product, sold under an existing brand. It can carry its own name and build a following of its own, and it is never sold alone.

In the house today: Campanion, under Campminder.

Feature

Folds into a platform we already run. No public brand of its own.

In the house today: None yet. Gazebo may be the first.

What does not count.

  • How attached anyone is to it.
  • How much work went into building it.
  • Who championed it.
  • What we paid to acquire it.

None of these tell us what a thing is worth to the portfolio, and all of them are easy to mistake for an answer.

Gazebo and Campanion are the first two use cases. UltraCamp follows once the customer research is in.

Calls that could get made by default get a decision record instead, so they are made by an owner on purpose.

05

The story inside.

One story, told the same way by sales, support, product, and leadership. It answers the question before it gets awkward. Three parts, and they go in this order.

01

Why Parent Co. exists.

Some people spend their lives building places where a community can gather. Summer camps where kids play and learn something about themselves. Swim teams where kids get to be athletes, for fun, for competition, or for a career that starts in a pool at seven in the morning. Running those places is hard, and the software has not always been good to them. Parent Co. exists because those people deserve tools that are a pleasure to use, and that keep getting better every year instead of getting older.

02

Why the brands are stronger together.

Parent Co. carries finance, staffing, engineering, and the parts of running a company that no single brand should have to solve twice. That is what lets a small team ship like a big one. Every brand punches above its weight because the smartest people at any one of them are working for all of them.

03

Why each brand stands on its own.

Each brand serves a group nobody else in the house serves. There is no overlap, and that is by design. A camp director and a swim coach do not want the same product and should not be sold the same one. Each brand is remarkable on its own terms, and each one is a real piece of a system that was thought all the way through.

TL;DR · the short version anyone can say out loud

We are one company behind several brands. Every one of them serves people who build places for their community. Camps. Swim teams. Programs where kids get to be kids.

Parent Co. carries the shared work so each brand can be great at the one thing it does. Each brand serves its own people under its own name, and none of them overlap. That is the whole idea.

Once this lands, it gets pressure tested three ways.

  1. What a rep says to a prospect who asks why Campminder and UltraCamp both exist.
  2. What a new hire learns in week one.
  3. What any of us says at a trade show.

06

Open items.

Five things are still open. None of them change the model. Each one decides what something is called, or who makes the call.

01

The parent company name.

The name is open. What Strategy settles is the architecture the parent lives inside, and how it relates to every other brand in the house. The name comes after that. A budget estimate for naming and identity comes in Activation. Parent Co. holds the place until then.

02

UltraCamp’s long term brand.

The call waits on UltraCamp customer research. Those interviews are ready to schedule. That way the decision gets made with customers instead of about them. Two things keep getting treated as one and should not be. Consolidating a codebase is a product decision. Keeping a name is a brand decision.

03

What Campanion is.

Two questions, in this order. Is it a companion app for Campminder, an app for any camp, or a product that could serve any vertical? Then, how visibly it carries the Campminder name. Discovery found that prospects often do not know it is Campminder at all.

04

Gazebo.

The rebuild points toward folding it in as a feature, named for what it does. That call runs through the framework in section 04, along with the communication that goes with it. It is the first real use case for the framework. Who Gazebo is for is still unsettled, and that answer comes first.

05

Who owns the brand.

This work crosses marketing, product, HR, sales, and client services, so it needs one accountable owner. The title matters less than the mandate. The playbook gets built around that person, which is why the role needs naming before Activation starts.
