01 Start Here

The Recommendation

A House of Brands. Campminder stays Campminder. A new parent carries the portfolio.

Each brand in the portfolio serves a different market and has equity worth protecting. The company already operates as a portfolio underneath the product names, with shared engineering, finance, and leadership.

A House of Brands gives that structure a name and makes it visible where it matters: hiring, investors, and every future acquisition.

Snug, Janet, and I arrived at this recommendation together and feel good about the direction. The full rationale, the four models I evaluated, and the discovery behind all of it are further down this page.

Decisions That Move Us Forward

Four decisions we need leadership to weigh in on. They’re the inputs that turn the recommendation into a working playbook.

01

Confirm the architecture.

A House of Brands is the direction we feel good about. Once it’s confirmed, naming the parent brand can start right away. The logo, identity, and a small parent website follow naturally from the name, all one process, and budget for it is already planned.

02

Which brands remain?

My working recommendation for each brand in the portfolio:

  • CampminderStays as a brand.
  • UltraCampStays as a brand. This is a brand decision, separate from the shared codebase work already planned.
  • GazeboBecomes a functionally named product feature, something like Groups.
  • CampanionNeeds assessment. Is it a companion app for Campminder, an app for all camps, or a product that could serve any vertical?
  • SwimtopiaStays as a brand.

What I need is your take on what to do with each brand, and what’s missing to make the decision on the ones we’re not sure about.

03

Who owns the brand, and what is the role?

This work crosses marketing, product, HR, sales, and client services, so it needs one accountable owner who can move quickly, communicate the vision, empower people on every team, and hold the brand as their charge rather than any one department’s agenda.

The title matters less than the mandate. It could sit with the incoming CMO, a dedicated brand manager, or a chief of staff position if one is created. What I need is the role named and its brand responsibilities defined, because the playbook gets built around that person.

04

What context are we missing?

Plans, constraints, or history that haven’t reached me yet, including product or acquisition plans that would change any of the answers above. Interviews with UltraCamp teammates and customers belong here too. They give us the context to handle UltraCamp well as a standalone brand, and they start to set the approach for evaluating the brands that arrive with future acquisitions. They’re ready to schedule once we align.

Initial Recommendation

Campminder needs to stay Campminder. Something broader needs to carry the portfolio.

The portfolio shown in a House of Brands model.

Illustrative. Individual brand decisions follow the discovery outlined below.

Below, three reasons a House of Brands is the right direction. The full comparison of the four models I evaluated is further down the page.

01

Campminder the product has real equity.

A House of Brands model protects the existing Campminder brand and platform. Customers keep the name they trust. The parent brand becomes the entity that signals quality and shared ownership without replacing what customers already know.

02

The portfolio serves truly different markets.

Within the current portfolio, Campminder customers and Swimtopia customers have different needs and different reasons to choose a product. As M&A continues, we’ll bring more platforms into the fold with unique positions. A House of Brands lets each brand speak directly to its own market without compromise.

03

This is how Campminder is already structured today.

The company already operates as a portfolio. It has shared engineering, shared finance, shared leadership, and shared infrastructure running beneath multiple product names. Giving that structure a real name and identity is not a reinvention. A House of Brands makes something already true more visible.

The strongest precedent for this at scale is Meta. When Facebook became Meta, the intention was clear: a parent brand that does not describe what any individual product does, but signals shared ownership, shared talent, and shared ambition. Individual products do the work of communicating what they are about. The parent brand makes the case for why they all come from the same place.

This kind of investment pays for itself. Kantar tracked brands over four years and found that those which improved their pricing power added 67% to their brand value, roughly twice the gain of everyone else. Brand value is not an abstraction. It shows up as market share, margin, and the ability to charge more. The structure has to be right before that growth can compound.

Next Steps

Four moves, in order. Each one sets up the next.

01

Interview UltraCamp customers.

Talk to UltraCamp customers before any decision is made about their brand. The goal is to learn how much the brand itself drives their loyalty, and what a transition story would need to sound like. If/when the codebase eventually sunsets, that story is coming either way. We need to own it.

02

Select the architecture model.

Confirm the model the portfolio will follow. My initial recommendation is a House of Brands. Whatever the final choice, the decision needs a clear owner and a decision framework for future acquisitions, so the answer stays consistent through every new deal.

03

Name the parent brand and align the identities.

Name the new parent company brand, then update the identities across the portfolio so they are cohesive and work together. Each brand keeps its own voice and its own audience. The system is what connects them.

04

Build the narratives.

Prepare the external narratives for product marketing, brand marketing, and customer lifecycle communication.

Alongside them, form the internal narrative so everyone on the team understands how the brands are interlinked and work together. This narrative is what a sales rep says when a prospect asks why UltraCamp and Campminder both exist, and what a new hire learns in their first week.

Branding Is Not Marketing

These two words get used interchangeably. They are not the same thing.

Marketing

External communication. Paid media, organic content, PR, advertising, events, and the website. Marketing tells people what you offer and why they should care.

Branding

The internal and external representation of the organization. The belief system that shapes how the company shows up: what it stands for, how it talks, how its products relate to each other, and how employees understand where they work and why it matters.

Marketing takes its direction from the brand. Without a clear brand, marketing becomes noise.

This distinction matters because the challenge surfaced in discovery is not a marketing problem. It’s a branding problem.

07

What Campminder Is Now

Campminder started as a product. One platform, one name, one audience. That was the right structure at the time.

Today, Campminder is a company that owns multiple products serving multiple markets.

We’ve acquired UltraCamp, Gazebo, and Swimtopia, and we built Campanion, which carries its own equity with the parents who use it. Most departments have grown exponentially, with financial, revenue, and client success operations that span more than one product name.

A product platform with a brand is managed one way. A company that owns many brands is managed another way. The structure, the communication, and the ownership of brand decisions all change when an organization crosses that line.

Campminder needs a brand foundation that works today and tomorrow. It’s time to catch up to what the organization represents now.

  • No two people interviewed described the relationship between Campminder and UltraCamp the same way.
  • Sales conversations require extra time to explain a portfolio that should explain itself.
  • New hires from acquired companies are not sure who they work for or who is on their team.
  • UltraCamp has operated without clear marketing ownership for nearly two years since the acquisition closed.

The Architecture Models

A brand architecture is the system that organizes how brands in a portfolio relate to each other. It determines how they show up together, how they borrow equity from one another, and how a customer or employee understands the whole from any single part.

There are four primary models. The strongest organizations pick one and commit to it. A clear architecture is easier for the people inside the building to follow and easier for people outside it to understand.

Branded House Not Recommended

One parent brand with clear connections to all products and services. Products often carry the parent brand name as part of their own. The parent brand does all the trust work.

Examples
Amazon, Apple, FedEx.
What works
Every new product or acquisition immediately inherits trust. Marketing investment compounds. The organization is easy to explain.
What it requires
The parent brand must be broad enough to carry everything under it without contradiction. A category-specific name creates a ceiling.
For Campminder
The Campminder name is tied to camp management software. Expanding it to carry swim management, group events, and future acquisitions in other verticals would stretch the name beyond what it can credibly hold.

Endorsed Brands Not Recommended

Individual brands that carry a visible connection to the parent. The endorsement can be a visual lockup, a written line, or a badge.

Examples
Marriott (Courtyard by Marriott, Westin by Marriott), Sony, Virgin.
What works
Individual brands get breathing room while still benefiting from parent brand trust.
What it requires
Clear rules for when and how the endorsement appears. A parent brand name compelling enough to be worth endorsing with.
For Campminder
Still requires a new parent brand name, plus consistent rules across every product. Adds visual complexity and ongoing governance.

Hybrid Not Recommended

A combination of the models above, where different brands in the portfolio have different levels of connection to the parent.

Examples
Amazon (Prime as branded house, Whole Foods as independent), Disney (Parks as branded house, Pixar and Marvel as endorsed), Coca-Cola, Alphabet (Google as a branded house, YouTube, Waymo, Nest).
What works
Flexibility for very large portfolios with truly diverse products and audiences.
What it requires
Significant governance. Different rules for different brands compounds over time.
For Campminder
This is close to what Campminder is doing now: different brands with different levels of connection and different explanations depending on who is asking. Hybrid architecture is more often an ad-hoc outcome of M&A activity than a deliberate choice. It tends to require more ongoing decision-making overhead than the alternatives.

House of Brands Recommended

A parent company that operates largely out of view. Each brand stands on its own with its own audience, positioning, and identity. The connection to the parent is minimal or invisible externally.

Examples
Meta (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp), P&G (Tide, Pampers, Gillette), PepsiCo. (food, drinks, restaurants)
What works
Each brand speaks directly to its own market without compromise. Acquisitions retain their equity. The parent takes no reputational risk from any individual product.
What it requires
A parent company name that can carry the full portfolio. Resources to support each brand in its own market.
For Campminder
A new parent company name, separate from the Campminder product, that signals shared ownership and shared ambition across the full portfolio. Campminder stays Campminder. The parent brand carries the hiring story, the investor story, and the acquisition story. Individual brands communicate directly with their own customers.

What We Don’t Know Yet

A final recommendation requires more information than I currently have. Three questions remain open.

01

How do UltraCamp customers feel about the acquisition?

Leadership raised this directly in interviews: no decision of this size should be made without hearing from the UltraCamp client base. Some customers might be thrilled. Some might not want to work with Campminder.

Most decisions about the UltraCamp brand so far have been made based on internal sentiment and assumptions about what customers care about. Any future changes to the platform experience or the branding means that a transition story is coming regardless. The question is whether that story lands well or creates churn. Talking to even a small sample of UltraCamp customers before making a brand recommendation will give us the context and insight to make a smart decision.

02

What’s the difference between a product decision and a brand decision?

Internally, these are being treated as the same thing. They are not. A codebase can be consolidated without a brand changing. A brand can persist even as the underlying technology is rebuilt.

When people at Campminder say “UltraCamp may go away,” they typically mean the engineering infrastructure will be unified. That does not automatically mean the external-facing brand disappears. These decisions need to be separated before the external communication begins, because confusing them has real consequences for how the transition lands with customers.

The same principle applies to Campanion. The question of what Campanion is as an external brand is separate from whatever decisions the engineering team makes about how it functions internally. Brand decisions should not be driven by technical architecture. They should be driven by what is clearest and most valuable for customers.

03

Where does brand management live?

The most consistent finding across every interview: ask who owns brand decisions and you get a different answer every time. Different people point to different executives. That internal question of ownership may be the biggest problem to solve.

A brand portfolio manager owns the internal and external story of the organization, sets the rules for how brands relate to each other, and decides how new acquisitions are integrated. This is a senior role, not a marketing role, and it is not the CMO. It requires authority across the organization. A playbook does not run itself.

For the same reason, the playbook can’t just be a skill in Claude. It is only as current as the information feeding it, and today that information lives in silos. Decisions made on stale or partial inputs compound quickly. Keeping the playbook current is part of the job.
